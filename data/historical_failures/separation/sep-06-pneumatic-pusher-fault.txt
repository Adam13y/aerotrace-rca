Title: Pneumatic pusher fault during separation
Category: separation
Failure mode: Pneumatic pusher fault
Source type: synthetic
Case ID: sep-06-pneumatic-pusher-fault
Note: Synthetic engineering summary of a failure mode, not a record of a specific event.

FAILURE MODE
Some vehicles use pneumatic actuators instead of pyrotechnic devices to release latches and push stages, fairings or payloads apart. A pressurised gas, often helium or nitrogen, drives pistons that provide the separation impulse. A fault means one or more pushers deliver too little force, act too late, or act unequally, giving low separation velocity or a tip-off moment.

CAUSAL CHAIN
The first deviation is usually upstream of the motion: the pneumatic supply pressure is low before the event because of a slow leak or regulator drift, or a valve opens late. When the command is issued, the pressure in that pusher rises slowly or to a lower peak. The stroke is shortened or delayed, so separation velocity is reduced and, if pushers act unequally, a rotation rate appears. Low relative velocity raises the risk of recontact once upper-stage ignition or residual thrust acts.

TELEMETRY SIGNATURES
- Pneumatic supply or bottle pressure lower than predicted before the separation command, or decaying over minutes
- Pusher pressure rise after the valve command slower or lower than nominal on one or more actuators
- Separation switches change state later than nominal and at different times around the circumference
- Relative separation velocity lower than predicted and attitude rate appearing after release

ROOT CAUSE
Typical causes are seal leakage at low temperature, contamination in valves or regulators, a mis-set regulator, an undersized gas reserve that does not cover cold-soak pressure loss, and piston friction or binding from debris or lubricant changes. Failure to telemeter pneumatic pressure makes slow leaks invisible until the event.

HISTORICAL REFERENCE
No single public incident is cited for this summary; it describes a generic failure mode recognised in launch-vehicle engineering practice. Pneumatic separation systems are valued because they are reusable and testable, but they depend on gas supply integrity throughout flight.

LESSONS LEARNED
- Telemeter supply and actuator pressures so a slow leak is visible before the event
- Size gas reserves for worst-case cold soak and leakage
- Test pushers at flight temperature and confirm symmetry of stroke timing across all actuators
