Title: High-pressure pneumatic line rupture
Category: pressurization
Failure mode: Pneumatic line rupture
Source type: synthetic
Case ID: press-06-pneumatic-line-rupture
Note: Synthetic engineering summary of a failure mode, not a record of a specific event.

FAILURE MODE
Pneumatic line rupture is the sudden failure of a high-pressure gas line, fitting or flex hose that supplies valve actuation gas or pressurant. A rupture releases gas quickly, producing a pressure collapse in the affected circuit and possibly mechanical damage or local cooling of nearby hardware. Pneumatic circuits typically operate at tens to hundreds of bar.

CAUSAL CHAIN
Initiators include vibration fatigue of a poorly supported line, chafing against structure, overpressure surges, or weld and fitting defects. The first observable is a rapid pressure drop in the affected pneumatic manifold, reaching near-ambient within a fraction of a second to a few seconds depending on line size, together with a step drop in supply bottle pressure and a bottle temperature decrease from rapid blowdown. Nearby accelerometers may register a transient at rupture. Valves depending on that supply then fail to actuate or move to their fail-safe positions; this can close main propellant valves, causing loss of thrust, or prevent commanded sequences such as staging or shutdown.

TELEMETRY SIGNATURES
- Abrupt pneumatic manifold pressure drop is the first anomaly, falling toward ambient within seconds
- Supply bottle pressure step decrease and bottle temperature drop from rapid blowdown
- Local accelerometer or acoustic transient at the time of rupture
- Valves on the affected circuit report fail-safe positions or fail to respond to commands
- Engine shutdown or sequence abort shortly afterwards

ROOT CAUSE
Root causes include inadequate clamping that leaves line natural frequencies within the vibration environment, defects in welds or brazed joints, incorrect fitting torque, chafing, and pressure surges from fast-opening valves. Lack of vibration testing on flight-configuration routing is a common contributor.

HISTORICAL REFERENCE
No single public incident is cited for this summary; it describes a generic failure mode recognised in launch-vehicle engineering practice. It is most often associated with vibration qualification gaps in line routing and support.

LESSONS LEARNED
- Support pneumatic lines so their natural frequencies avoid dominant vibration environments
- Inspect welds and brazed joints with radiography or an equivalent method
- Design pneumatically actuated valves with safe failure positions and isolate pneumatic circuits
