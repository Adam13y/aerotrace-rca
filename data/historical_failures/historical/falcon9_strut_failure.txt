Title: Falcon 9 CRS-7 - second-stage LOX tank overpressure from a failed helium bottle strut
Category: structural
Failure mode: COPV support strut failure causing helium release and LOX tank overpressure
Source type: historical
Case ID: falcon9_strut_failure
Date: 28 June 2015
Vehicle: SpaceX Falcon 9 (CRS-7 cargo mission)
Sources: SpaceX CRS-7 investigation update (July 2015); NASA Independent Review Team summary (2018)

FAILURE MODE
The second-stage liquid oxygen (LOX) tank overpressurised and burst about 139 seconds into flight, while the first stage was still firing, after a high-pressure helium bottle mounted inside the tank broke loose. The vehicle broke up and the Dragon cargo spacecraft bound for the International Space Station was lost.

CAUSAL CHAIN
Helium pressurant for the second stage is stored in composite overwrapped pressure vessels (COPVs) mounted inside the LOX tank with steel struts. Under the high axial acceleration of late first-stage flight (around 3.2 g), the buoyant helium bottle pulls hard on its struts. SpaceX's investigation found that one strut most likely failed at roughly 2,000 lbf, far below its 10,000 lbf rating. The bottle broke free, its plumbing failed, and high-pressure helium flooded the LOX tank faster than the relief system could vent it. The tank overpressurised and burst. From the first sign of trouble to loss of telemetry was under one second. The Dragon capsule survived the breakup and continued transmitting for some time, but its software at the time did not include a contingency mode to deploy its parachutes, so it was lost on impact with the ocean. NASA's Independent Review Team agreed that the strut failure was a credible cause, and also attributed the failure to a design error: the use of an industrial-grade cast part in a critical load path without adequate screening, testing or margin.

TELEMETRY SIGNATURES
- A sudden pressure drop in the second-stage helium pressurisation system.
- At the same moment, a rapid rise in second-stage LOX tank ullage pressure: opposite-sign changes in a pressurant source and the tank it feeds.
- Timing during powered flight near peak axial acceleration, not at a planned transient such as staging or throttling.
- An extremely short timeline (well under one second) from first anomaly to loss of signal.

ROOT CAUSE
SpaceX identified the most probable cause as structural failure of a COPV support strut under flight loads, releasing helium into the LOX tank. The NASA review team considered the strut a credible cause but noted that other contributing failure paths could not be fully excluded, and emphasised the design and part-qualification decisions. Corrective actions included replacing the struts and qualifying parts individually.

LESSONS LEARNED
- Simultaneous opposite-sign pressure changes in connected volumes indicate an internal leak path between them.
- Structural failures of internal hardware tend to occur at peak load; check the acceleration profile at the anomaly time.
- When the timeline to loss of signal is sub-second, high-rate data near the anomaly is critical; low-rate telemetry may show only the end state.
- Contingency modes for payload recovery should be considered for failures outside the planned sequence.
